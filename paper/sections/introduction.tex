\section{Introduction}

Choosing an appropriate inductive bias is crucial for generalization \cite{baxter2000model}.
By constraining the hypothesis space to reflect the underlying task structure, such a bias enables effective learning from limited data.
In neural networks, examples of structural inductive biases include local connectivity and spatial weight sharing in convolutional architectures \cite{lecun1998gradient}, and recurrent state updates with parameters shared across time in recurrent architectures \cite{elman1990finding}.
Suitable parameter-sharing patterns also enforce equivariance to task-relevant transformations \cite{ravanbakhsh2017equivariance}.
Building on these principles, recent work has explored learning symmetry-inducing parameter-sharing patterns directly from data \cite{zhou2021meta,yeh2022equivariance}.


Selecting suitable structural biases by hand requires domain expertise, particularly when the relevant properties of a new task are not known in advance \cite{yeh2022equivariance}.
This motivates learning such biases from data.
For example, \citet{zhou2021meta} represent layer weights as the product of a transformation matrix shared across tasks and a vector of task-specific parameters.
The shared matrix is meta-learned across tasks to capture transferable parameter-sharing patterns, including those that induce equivariance.
For larger layers, the authors use a Kronecker-factorized approximation to reduce memory and computational costs, restricting the search to a structured family of reparameterizations.
More generally, a single task may admit multiple near-optimal structures, and its data alone need not identify which constraints will transfer to other tasks.
The broader challenge is therefore to identify structural constraints that support learning across an environment of related tasks and generalize to previously unseen tasks from that environment \cite{baxter2000model}.
